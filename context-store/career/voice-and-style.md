# Voice & Style Guide

Captures how Ryan writes and speaks professionally. BrainOS `apply` reads this to match tone in cover letters and emails.

---

## Tone

Direct and structured. Lead with the conclusion, then support it with evidence. No buildup.

I write like I think. Short, clear, outcome-focused. I do not perform confidence or hedge unnecessarily. If something is unclear or broken, I say it directly and explain why.

I balance confidence with proof. Claims are backed by metrics, systems, or observed patterns. If evidence is mixed, I say that explicitly instead of smoothing it over.

No corporate tone. No filler. Every sentence should move the argument forward.

---

## Recurring themes and perspectives

- Most product problems are not execution problems. They are system problems:
  - decision rights are unclear  
  - outcomes are not measured  
  - roadmap and architecture drift apart  

- AI is not magic. It collapses coordination and exposes broken systems before it creates advantage.

- Product management is about **decision systems**, not roadmaps or features.

- Activity is not progress. Output is not outcome. Most organizations optimize for the wrong signal.

- Platform work is misunderstood. Real platform work is:
  - defining system boundaries  
  - aligning incentives  
  - enabling others to build and ship  

- I am drawn to stalled or misaligned systems. That is where leverage exists.

- Good PMs do not just prioritize. They:
  - define what matters  
  - align ownership  
  - remove constraints  

- Speed without structure creates friction. Structure without ownership creates paralysis.

---

## What to avoid

- No corporate speak:
  - avoid “leverage”, “synergy”, “drive alignment”, “cross-functional excellence”

- No passive voice:
  - always specify who did what and why

- No empty enthusiasm:
  - avoid “I’m excited to…” unless tied to a specific reason

- No hedging:
  - avoid “I think”, “I believe” unless uncertainty is real and relevant

- No buzzwords without meaning:
  - AI, platform, innovation must be grounded in actual systems or outcomes

- No long-winded setup:
  - do not bury the point under context

- No performative storytelling:
  - if a story does not show a decision and an outcome, cut it

## Sample writing

Most platform programs stall for the same reason. Decision rights break down. Outcomes stop being measured. Roadmap and architecture drift apart. At that point, teams stay busy but stop shipping. Adding more process does not fix it. It increases coordination cost.

At Bank of America, the issue was not execution. It was ownership. Security operations, engineering, and compliance were all involved, but no one had clear decision authority. I restructured decision rights across those groups and separated research from production workflows. That removed about $60K per month in coordination overhead and restored predictable delivery.

AI does not fix this. It accelerates it. It makes teams faster at execution while the underlying system is still misaligned. That increases friction, not output. The advantage comes after the system is fixed, not before.

Most teams treat onboarding as a services problem. That is why it does not scale. At Splunk, onboarding took six weeks and required heavy support. I rebuilt it as a product surface with clear activation steps and measurable outcomes. Time to value dropped to three weeks with the same team and no additional headcount.

The pattern is consistent. When outcomes are unclear and ownership is diffuse, work expands but progress stalls. When you fix the system, execution follows.
